# Candidate Skills

Skills worth writing, and the reasoning for each. Not a backlog to burn down — a skill
written to clear an entry here is a skill written from imagination, which is the failure
mode `authoring-skills` warns about. These wait until real friction says they are needed.

Each entry names what it would say, why it is not covered by what exists, and what would
make it hard to write well.

---

### stop-conditions

**The idea.** Knowing which of three states you are in: done, blocked, or working on the
wrong problem. Agents are poor at all three — they continue past done, work around blocked
rather than reporting it, and almost never conclude the problem itself was misframed.

**Why it is not covered.** `convergent-planning` says where the checkpoints go;
`implement` says stop rather than compensate. Neither says how to tell that *now* is a
stopping point, or which kind. The mirror of convergent planning: that skill is about
arriving at checkpoints, this one is about reading them.

**The hard part.** "Know when to stop" is a platitude unless it names the specific
tells. The content is the tells: the fourth workaround in a row, the check that keeps
being redefined downward, the growing gap between what is being built and what was asked
for. Without those it is filler.

**Priority.** First. Sharpest of the four and pairs directly with `convergent-planning`.

---

### taste

**The idea.** Telling good from adequate. When "it works" is not the bar and why, what
makes one solution better than another that passes the same tests, and how to argue for
quality without appealing to preference.

**Why it is not covered.** `code-review` asks what a change will cost later — a
consequentialist test. This is the aesthetic one, which is different and harder: some
things are worth doing because they are right, and that judgement needs its own grounding.

**The hard part.** The most likely to come out as vapour. It needs to survive the test
of saying something that would not have been done anyway — otherwise it is a page of
"prefer elegant code". Probably the most personal of the four, and the one that most needs
to be written from your actual reactions rather than from principles.

**Priority.** Highest value, highest risk. Worth attempting once there are notes on real
cases where adequate was rejected and why.

---

### hold-the-thread

**The idea.** Working across compaction, interruption, and handover. What must be written
down as you go because it cannot be reconstructed, versus what is cheap to re-derive. The
discipline of leaving state that a cold reader can resume from.

**Why it is not covered.** `investigate-debug` has a version of this scoped to
investigations. The general case is broader: it applies to any long task, and the
judgement — what survives, what is disposable — is the same everywhere.

**The hard part.** Avoiding the note-taking-procedure trap. The wisdom is in the
*selection* (conclusions and eliminations survive; transcripts do not), not in a template.
The old `investigate-debug` failed exactly here, with fill-in-the-blank forms.

**Priority.** Third. Real and general, but partially served today.

---

### the-user-is-thinking-out-loud

**The idea.** Telling a request from a musing. Someone circling an idea wants the space
opened; someone with a task wants it executed. Treating the first as the second forecloses
the thinking, which is the more expensive error.

**Why it is not covered.** Possibly it is. `map-the-space` covers the response to a
musing, and its trigger already includes "circling an idea rather than executing one" and
requests whose framing looks inherited.

**The hard part.** Deciding whether this is a skill at all, or one sharper paragraph
inside `map-the-space`. Splitting a trigger from its response usually makes both weaker.

**Priority.** Last. Resolve by improving `map-the-space` first and seeing what is left.

---

## Notes on writing any of these

The bar is `surface-forks`: a reframe that changes how the situation is seen, concrete
enough to act on, with failure modes that name traps invisible from inside. See
`plugins/terma/skills/authoring-skills/SKILL.md`.

Two tests before starting. Has the friction actually occurred, more than once? And would
the content have been produced anyway without the skill? A yes to the second means there
is nothing to write yet.
